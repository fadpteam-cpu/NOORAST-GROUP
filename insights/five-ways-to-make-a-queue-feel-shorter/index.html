Five ways to make a queue feel shorter

The wait is part of the visit. These are the changes that alter how long it feels.

Tell the truth about the wait

A posted time that is shorter than the queue feels dishonest, and guests say so. An honest wait time, updated as the line moves, is more useful than a low number.

If the operation cannot yet post a reliable time, say less, not a figure that will be wrong.

Give guests something to look at

A blank fence and a view of the backs of other people make a wait feel longer. Sightlines to the attraction, a scene, or a simple piece of theming give the queue a purpose.

This does not require a new building. It requires something at eye level that changes as the line moves.

Shade, shelter and somewhere to lean

Heat, rain and nowhere to rest are physical, and they show up in reviews. Shade, a roof where the wait is longest, and a rail at a height people can lean on change the same queue.

These are often the first items on a refurbishment drawing.

Show progress

Guests tolerate a wait better when they can see that they are moving. Switchbacks that hide the front of the queue, or a single long line with no markers, feel slower than they are.

The layout of the railings is a design decision, not only an operations one.

Look at the whole day

One well-designed queue does not fix a day in which every wait is unexplained. The Guest Experience Review looks at the sequence: arrival, the longest wait, food, the late afternoon and the exit.

Shortening the feeling of one queue is useful. Knowing which queue decides the visit is more useful.
